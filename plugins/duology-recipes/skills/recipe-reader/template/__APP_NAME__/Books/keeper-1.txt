The supply boat came on the second Tuesday of October, as it always had, and Elin Marr watched it from the gallery long before she could hear its engine. It was a small grey shape at first, then a white wake, then a man in an orange coat standing in the bow with a coil of rope over his shoulder.

She went down the hundred and twelve steps of the tower without counting them. Twenty-six years on Gull Rock had put the number into her knees. At the landing stage the wind came round the point and pushed the water into the slipway in long, slapping lines, and she waited with her hands in her pockets while Tomas brought the boat alongside.

“Last one before the winter,” he called. “And last one for you, if the letter means what it says.”

The letter meant what it said. She had read it four times in September and once more that morning. The light on Gull Rock would be fitted with a lamp that switched itself on at dusk and off at dawn, and a battery that a man from the mainland would visit twice a year. There would be no need for a keeper. The board thanked her for her long and careful service, and hoped she would find the arrangements for her departure in order.

They unloaded the boxes together. Paraffin she would not need, out of habit, because the order had gone in before the letter came. Flour, oats, tinned milk, a sack of potatoes, a crate of apples from the farm behind the harbour. A parcel of newspapers tied with string. Tomas carried the heaviest box up the path to the cottage and set it on the kitchen table, and then he stood in the doorway with his cap in his hands as if he had come to a funeral.

“You could come back with me now,” he said. “There’s room. They wouldn’t mind.”

“They wouldn’t notice,” Elin said. “That’s different.”

She made tea, because there was always tea, and they drank it standing up. Through the window the sea was the colour of slate, and the gulls that gave the rock its name sat in rows along the wall of the yard with their feathers turned against the wind. Tomas told her about the harbour: the new café, the school that had lost its roof in the August storm, his daughter who was learning to sail and already thought she knew more than he did.

“She probably does,” Elin said, and he laughed.

When he had gone she stood on the landing stage until the boat was a white wake again, then a grey shape, then nothing at all. The tide was turning. She could feel it in the way the weed on the rocks lifted and settled, lifted and settled, like something breathing in its sleep.

In the afternoon she did what she had done every afternoon for twenty-six years. She climbed the tower and cleaned the lens. It was a great cage of glass prisms, taller than she was, and it took an hour to wipe every face of it with a soft cloth and a little spirit. She trimmed nothing, because there had been no wick to trim since the lamp went electric in her ninth year, but she checked the bulb, the spare bulb, the motor that turned the lens, and the brass gears that would turn it by hand if the motor failed. She wrote the time in the log, and the weather, and the words: all in order.

Then she sat in the gallery with her back against the warm wall of the lamp room and looked at the sea.

People on the mainland had always asked her what she did all day, and she had never found a way to answer that satisfied them. She kept the light. She kept the log. She kept the paint on the railings and the grease on the gears and the gulls off the solar panels that someone had bolted to the roof of the store. She watched the weather come in from the west and wrote it down. She read. In the evenings she listened to the shipping forecast on the radio, the names of the sea areas going by like the stations of a long railway, and she knew which boats were out and roughly where.

But that was not really what she did. What she did was pay attention. A light on a rock is a promise that someone is paying attention, and she had believed for a long time that the promise only meant something if it was true.

The new lamp would keep the promise without anyone behind it. She did not know yet whether that made the promise smaller or larger. She suspected it made it a different kind of promise, the kind a clock makes, and she had never had much time for clocks.

At dusk she switched the light on by hand, for what she guessed would be one of the last forty or fifty times. The beam swung out over the water, white, then dark, then white again, every fifteen seconds. Far out to the south a fishing boat answered with two short blasts of its horn, the way some of them still did, and she raised her hand although no one could possibly see it.
