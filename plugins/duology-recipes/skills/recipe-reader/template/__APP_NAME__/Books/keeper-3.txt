The engineers came in the first week of December, three of them, in a launch that smelled of diesel and new paint. They were polite and quick and very young, and they called her Mrs Marr although she had never been married, and she did not correct them because it did not seem worth the trouble.

They spent two days in the tower. They fitted a new lamp no bigger than a loaf of bread, a row of batteries in a steel cabinet, a small grey box with a blinking green light that would tell someone on the mainland whether all was well. They did not take out the great glass lens. It was too heavy and too old to move, the eldest of them said, and anyway it still did its job. The little lamp would sit inside it, where the old bulb had been, and the prisms would gather its light and throw it out across the water just as they always had.

“So it’s the same light,” Elin said.

“Same flash, same range,” the engineer said. “Nobody at sea will know the difference.”

She thought about that for a long time afterwards. Nobody at sea will know the difference. He had meant it as a comfort, and she supposed it was one. The man and his son in the yacht had counted a flash every fifteen seconds and known where they were. They would have counted the same flash from the new lamp and known the same thing. The promise would hold.

But she would know the difference, and so, she suspected, would the rock.

On her last evening the engineers had gone back to the mainland and the new lamp was due to switch itself on at dusk. Elin climbed the tower at four o’clock with a flask of tea and sat in the gallery to watch it happen. The sky to the west was clear and yellow, fading to green at the top, and the sea was so calm that the gulls sat on it in rafts, rising and falling on a swell too long to see.

She had cleaned the lens that afternoon, every prism, an hour with the soft cloth and the spirit. Nobody had asked her to. The man from the mainland would come twice a year and would probably clean it then, or would probably not. She had written in the log, for the last time: all in order. Then she had closed it and put it in her bag to take with her, because the board had said she could keep it, and because she could not imagine leaving it in a drawer for someone to throw away.

At eleven minutes past four the sun touched the line of the sea. At sixteen minutes past it was half gone, a red arch on the water. At twenty past it was gone altogether, and the yellow sky began to darken from the top.

She found she was holding her breath.

At twenty-six minutes past four the small grey box gave a quiet click, and the light came on.

It came on the way the old one had, not suddenly but with a kind of gathering, the prisms catching it and passing it from one to the next until the whole lens was full of it. Then the beam swung out over the water, white, then dark, then white again, every fifteen seconds, exactly as it had every night of her life on the rock. Far out to the south a fishing boat gave two short blasts of its horn, and Elin, who had not meant to, raised her hand.

She sat there until it was full dark. The beam went round and round above her head and she counted it, one, two, three, up to fifteen, the way she had counted it in the fog. It did not need her. It had never needed her, she thought, not in the way she had believed. It had needed someone to pay attention, and for twenty-six years that had been her, and now it would be the sailors themselves, every one of them counting in the dark.

That was not a smaller promise. It was the same promise, passed on.

In the morning Tomas came with the boat. She carried her two bags down the path and he carried the box of books, and they did not say much. At the end of the landing stage she turned and looked up at the tower, white against a white sky, the lens catching the low sun and throwing it back at her like a signal.

“You’ll miss it,” Tomas said.

“I’ll see it,” Elin said. “From the harbour, every fifteen seconds. That’s not the same as missing it.”

He started the engine, and the rock got smaller, and the gulls rose from the wall of the yard all at once and wheeled out over the water behind them, calling, as if they had something to say and had only just remembered what it was.
