A dough left alone is not idle. In the first hour the flour finishes drinking the water, and the gluten begins to line up on its own, without any kneading at all. If you pinch the dough at this point it tears. Come back in an hour and it stretches.

This is why a slow dough needs so little work. Kneading is a way of building gluten quickly, by force. Time builds it gently, by itself. All you have to do is help it along with a few folds in the first two hours, then stay out of the way.

A fold takes a minute. Wet your hand, slide it under one side of the dough, lift that side up until it stretches, and lay it over the top. Turn the bowl a quarter turn and do it again, four times in all, until you have gone round the whole dough. Cover the bowl. Each set of folds makes the dough a little smoother and a little stronger. Three sets, half an hour apart, are plenty. If you forget one, nothing bad happens.

After the last fold, the dough rises. This is the part of the recipe that most people find hardest, because nothing seems to happen for hours, and then everything happens at once.

At a cool room temperature, around twenty degrees, a dough with one gram of yeast takes ten to fourteen hours to double. In a warm kitchen it can take eight. In a cold one it can take eighteen. The clock is a guide, never a rule. Watch the dough, not the time.

A dough that is ready has grown to about twice its size, has a domed top with a few bubbles at the surface, and wobbles when you shake the bowl, like a set jelly. If you dip a wet finger into it, the hole stays. A dough that is not ready is flat-topped and firm and springs back at once. A dough that has gone too far has collapsed a little in the middle, smells sharply of alcohol, and feels slack and sticky.

Even an over-risen dough will usually make bread. It will be flatter and its crust paler, because the yeast has eaten more of the sugars that brown the crust. Bake it anyway, and next time give it less time or a cooler spot. Every loaf teaches you something about your kitchen.

Many bakers use the refrigerator to make the timing easier. Mix the dough after dinner, give it its folds, leave it on the counter for two or three hours to get going, then put the covered bowl in the fridge. Cold slows the yeast almost to a stop, but not quite. The dough will keep rising, very slowly, for a day or even two, and it will gain flavour the whole time. When you want to bake, take it out and let it finish rising at room temperature.

Once the dough has risen, you shape it. Tip it onto a lightly floured counter, top side down. Gently pull the edges out into a rough square, then fold the sides into the middle like a letter, and roll it up from the near edge to the far one. Turn it seam side down and, with your hands curved around it, drag it towards you across the counter a few times. The bottom will catch on the counter and the top will tighten into a smooth skin.

That skin matters. It holds the shape of the loaf while it rises a second time, and it gives the oven something to push against. A loose, baggy loaf spreads sideways. A tight one rises up.

Put the shaped loaf in a bowl lined with a floured cloth, seam side up, and cover it. This second rise is short, an hour or so at room temperature, because the yeast is already awake and well fed. The loaf is ready when you press it gently with a floured finger and the dent fills back in slowly, but not all the way. If it springs back at once, wait a little longer. If it does not come back at all, bake it now.

While the loaf has its second rise, heat the oven. That is where the last chapter begins.
