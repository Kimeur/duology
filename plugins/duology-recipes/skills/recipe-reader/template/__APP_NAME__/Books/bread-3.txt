A home oven is a poor bread oven. It is small, it leaks heat every time you open it, and it is dry. Professional bread ovens are heavy with stone or steel and full of steam for the first minutes of baking, and that steam is what gives their loaves a thin, crackling crust that blisters as it cools.

The simplest way to give your loaf the same start is a pot with a lid. A heavy cast iron casserole is ideal, but any oven-safe pot with a close-fitting lid will do. With the lid on, the loaf bakes in its own steam for the first half of the bake. With the lid off, it dries and browns.

Put the empty pot, lid and all, in the oven and heat it to two hundred and fifty degrees Celsius for at least half an hour. The pot needs to be as hot as the oven, all the way through, so that the loaf meets a burst of heat from below the moment it goes in.

When the loaf has finished its second rise, cut a sheet of baking paper a little bigger than the loaf. Turn the loaf out of its bowl onto the paper, so that the smooth top is now on top. With a sharp knife or a razor blade, cut a slash across the top, about a centimetre deep, in one quick, confident stroke. This cut gives the loaf a place to open as it springs up in the heat. Without it, the loaf will split wherever its skin is weakest, usually along one side.

Take the hot pot out of the oven and set it on the hob or a heatproof board. Take off the lid. Holding the corners of the paper, lower the loaf into the pot, paper and all. Put the lid back on. Put the pot back in the oven, and turn the heat down to two hundred and thirty degrees.

Bake it for twenty minutes with the lid on. This is when the loaf does its last rising, the burst that bakers call oven spring. The heat makes the gas in the dough expand and the last of the water turn to steam, and the loaf can grow by a third in ten minutes. The lid keeps the surface moist so the crust stays soft and stretchy while all this happens.

Then take the lid off. The loaf will be pale and risen, with the slash opened into a wide, rough-edged ear. Bake it for another twenty to twenty-five minutes without the lid, until the crust is a deep, even brown, darker than you think it should be. Pale crusts taste of very little. Dark ones taste of nuts, caramel and toast. If you are nervous, err on the side of dark. It is very hard to burn a loaf in this time at this heat, and very easy to underbake one.

To check that it is done, lift the loaf out by the paper and knock on the bottom with a knuckle. A baked loaf sounds hollow, like a knock on a door. If you have a thermometer, the middle of the crumb should read at least ninety-six degrees.

Now comes the hardest instruction in this guide. Let the loaf cool on a rack for at least an hour before you cut it.

The bread is still baking as it cools. The crumb is full of steam that has not finished setting the starch, and if you cut it hot, the knife will drag the soft middle into a gummy streak and let out the steam that should have stayed in. Wait, and the crumb will set into the open, springy texture you worked for. As it cools, the crust will crackle and sing, tiny cracks running across it like ice on a pond. That sound is one of the small rewards of baking, and it is free.

Keep the loaf cut side down on a board. It will stay good for two or three days, and after that it will make very good toast. Do not keep it in the refrigerator, which makes bread go stale faster. If you will not finish it, slice it and freeze the slices, and toast them straight from the freezer.

And then, when the last slice is gone, mix another dough in the evening, and leave it alone.
