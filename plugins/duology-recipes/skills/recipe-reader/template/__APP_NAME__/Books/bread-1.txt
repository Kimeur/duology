Good bread needs four things, and one of them is time. The other three are flour, water and salt. Yeast matters too, but you need very little of it, and if you give the dough enough time, even that small amount will do all the work you ask of it.

This guide is for people who bake at home, around a working week, with an ordinary oven. It does not ask you to keep a starter alive, buy special tins or wake at four in the morning. It asks you to mix a dough in the evening, leave it alone, and bake it the next day. Most of the skill is in leaving it alone.

Start with flour. Bread flour, sometimes sold as strong flour, has more protein than the flour you use for cakes. When it meets water, two of those proteins join into gluten, a stretchy web that traps the gas the yeast makes. More protein means a stronger web and a taller loaf. You can make bread with plain flour, and it will taste good, but it will spread more and rise less.

Whole wheat flour brings flavour and colour, but the bran in it cuts the gluten web like tiny blades. If you want to use it, start by swapping a fifth of the white flour for whole wheat, see how the loaf behaves, and go up from there. A loaf that is half whole wheat is a fine thing. A loaf that is all whole wheat is a different project, and a heavier one.

Weigh your flour. A cup of flour can weigh anything from a hundred and twenty to a hundred and sixty grams, depending on how you fill it, and that difference is the difference between a slack dough and a stiff one. A cheap kitchen scale will make your bread more reliable than any other tool you can buy.

Next, water. Bakers describe a dough by its hydration: the weight of the water as a share of the weight of the flour. Five hundred grams of flour and three hundred and fifty grams of water is seventy percent hydration. That is a good place to begin. The dough will be soft and a little sticky, but you will be able to handle it with wet hands.

Wetter doughs, eighty percent and above, make an open, glossy crumb with big uneven holes, the kind you see in photographs. They are also harder to shape and quicker to spread. Drier doughs, sixty percent and below, are easy to handle and make a tighter, more even crumb that is good for sandwiches. Neither is better. Choose the bread you want to eat.

Use water that is cool or barely warm, never hot. A slow dough does not need help to get going, and warm water speeds the yeast up just when you want it to take its time. In summer, use water straight from the tap. In a cold winter kitchen, take the chill off it.

Then salt. Salt is not only for taste, although bread without it tastes flat and strangely sweet. Salt also tightens the gluten and slows the yeast a little, which keeps a long rise under control. Use two percent of the flour’s weight: ten grams for five hundred grams of flour. Fine sea salt dissolves fastest. If you only have coarse salt, crush it first or dissolve it in a little of the water.

Finally, yeast. For a dough that rises overnight, you need far less than most recipes say. One gram of instant yeast, about a quarter of a teaspoon, is enough for five hundred grams of flour if the dough has twelve to sixteen hours. It looks like nothing. It is plenty. Yeast multiplies as it feeds, and a long, slow rise gives it all the time it needs to fill the dough with gas and with flavour.

That flavour is the real reason to wait. In a quick dough, the yeast makes gas and little else. In a slow one, the yeast and the bacteria that come along with the flour have hours to break starch into sugars and sugars into dozens of small, interesting compounds. The crust browns more deeply. The crumb tastes of wheat instead of yeast. A slow loaf made with four ingredients can taste richer than a quick one made with ten.

So the recipe this guide returns to, again and again, is short. Five hundred grams of bread flour. Three hundred and fifty grams of cool water. Ten grams of salt. One gram of instant yeast. Mix them in a bowl with your hand or a spoon until no dry flour is left, cover the bowl, and go and do something else.
