Dear Jonah,

The conductor knocked at a quarter to six, exactly as she promised, and said the border in a voice so gentle that for a moment I thought I had dreamed it. I put on my coat over my pyjamas and went into the corridor, and the whole carriage was doing the same, a row of rumpled people in coats and slippers standing at the windows with their hair on end.

There was nothing much to see at first. A long, low station with a flat roof, a row of lamps still lit against the grey, a sign in two languages. Two officers came through the train, one from each side, and looked at our passports and at our faces and handed the passports back with a small nod each. It took twenty minutes. Nobody spoke above a murmur, as if we were in a library, or a church.

Then the train moved off again, slowly, over a bridge, and I understood why the conductor had asked whether I wanted to be woken.

The river at the border is wide, much wider than ours, and the bridge is long and low and made of iron girders painted green. Under it the water was moving fast and very smooth, the colour of pewter, with mist lying on it in a thin white sheet. On the far bank there were willows, and behind the willows a long line of hills, and behind the hills the sky was turning from grey to the palest gold. As we crossed, the sun came up over the hills and laid a line of light right across the water, and the mist began to lift off the river in slow curls, like steam off a cup.

Everybody in the corridor went quiet. The old man with the violin was standing two windows down from me, and I saw him take off his glasses and wipe them, and put them back on, and keep looking.

It lasted perhaps two minutes. Then we were across, and the willows were going by, and a farmer on a tractor in a field beside the line raised his hand to the train, and the day had simply started, the way days do, without any fuss.

I have been trying since then to work out why it moved me so much. It was beautiful, of course, but I have seen beautiful sunrises before. I think it was the border itself. Yesterday, at the station, I was in the place where I live, with all its ordinary troubles. Last night I was nowhere in particular, in a moving room in the dark. And this morning I was somewhere new, and the new place began with that river, and that light, and a stranger waving from a tractor.

I know a border is only a line that people agreed to draw. The river does not care which side of it is which. The mist lifted off both banks at the same time. But a train makes you feel the line, because it carries you over it so slowly, and there is a moment in the middle of the bridge when you are on neither side, and that moment feels like a door.

Breakfast was in the paper bag: a soft roll, a small piece of cheese, an apple, and a tiny jar of apricot jam that I have kept because the label is so pretty. The conductor brought coffee in a real cup with a saucer that rattled all the way along the corridor. The violinist knocked on my door to say goodbye before his stop and told me he had decided to play the piece twice at the wedding, once for the couple and once for himself, and that if he cried the second time, that was nobody’s business.

We arrive in two hours. The fields outside are wide and flat now, with rows of tall poplars marking the edges, and every few minutes we pass a village with a church tower and a station too small for the train to stop at. I am going to pack my things, fold up the bed, and sit by the window until the city starts.

I promise you a postcard from the other end. In the meantime, here is the river, as well as I can give it to you. I hope it reaches you the way it reached me.

With love, from the other side,

Ada
