The fog came in on the ninth day after the boat, and it stayed for three.

It arrived the way it always did, not as weather but as an absence. In the morning the mainland was a low blue line under a white sky. By eleven the line had gone, and by noon the end of the landing stage had gone too, and Elin stood in the yard and could not see the wall of the store from the cottage door. The gulls stopped calling. The sea went on, but the sound of it changed, closer and softer, as if it had come up the rock to listen.

She switched on the fog signal at a quarter past twelve and wrote the time in the log. The signal was a horn on the north side of the tower that gave two long notes every half minute, and on a clear day it would carry eight miles. In fog, nobody could say how far it carried. Fog bends sound and swallows it and sometimes throws it a long way off in the wrong direction. The horn was not a map. It was a voice, saying only: there is a rock here, and someone knows it.

On the first night she did not sleep much. She sat in the kitchen with the radio low and the log open on the table, and every hour she climbed the tower to look at a sea she could not see. The beam went out into the fog and stopped, a solid white bar that seemed to end a few yards from the glass, as though the light had hit a wall. She knew it did not end there. Somewhere out in the grey, a man on a deck would see a dull glow come and go every fifteen seconds and would know, from the pattern, which glow it was.

That was the first thing they taught you, long ago, before the bulbs and the motors and the solar panels. Every light has its own character. Gull Rock was one white flash every fifteen seconds. The light on the headland to the north was two flashes every ten. The buoy on the sandbank to the east was a quick green flicker that never stopped. A sailor who could count could tell where he was in the dark without seeing anything but light.

The new lamp would flash the same way. She had asked about that, in her letter back to the board, and they had replied quite kindly that of course the character of the light would not change. It seemed to have surprised them that she had asked.

On the second day the fog thinned for an hour in the afternoon and she saw a shape go by to the west: a coaster, low in the water, feeling its way along the edge of the channel at half speed. She watched it until the fog closed again and then wrote it in the log. Coaster, westbound, approximately one mile, visibility poor. Nobody would read that line. Nobody had read most of the lines in that log for years. But it was true, and it was hers, and when she wrote it down the coaster became something that had happened instead of something she had merely seen.

On the second night the radio said that a yacht was overdue on its way to the harbour. Two people on board, a man and his grown son, last heard of near the sandbank in the morning. The coastguard asked all vessels to keep a lookout. Elin put on her coat and her boots and went up to the gallery and stayed there.

There was nothing to see. She knew there would be nothing to see. She stayed anyway, with her collar up and her hands on the cold rail, while the horn below her sounded its two long notes and the beam went out and stopped, went out and stopped. Around two in the morning the fog began to move, not lifting but sliding, great slow banks of it passing the tower like the sides of ships, and in a gap between two of them she saw a small light low on the water to the south-east. Red, then nothing. Red, then nothing.

A port light, swinging as a boat rolled.

She went down the hundred and twelve steps faster than she had gone down them in years and called the coastguard on the radio. She gave the bearing from the tower and her best guess at the distance, and the man on the other end repeated it back to her twice in a calm, flat voice. Twenty minutes later she heard an engine she did not know, a lifeboat coming up from the harbour, and saw its searchlight brush the underside of the fog.

They found the yacht at four. Its engine had failed and its radio with it, and the man and his son had been drifting since the afternoon, taking turns to sound a hand horn that nobody heard. They had seen her light, the son told the coastguard later, and counted it, and known it was Gull Rock, and so they had known they were not lost, only stuck. It had helped, he said. It had helped more than he could explain.

In the log, at four fifteen, she wrote: yacht located by lifeboat, two persons safe. Then, after a moment, she added: fog lifting.
