Dear Jonah,

I am writing this on the little fold-down table in my compartment, which is exactly the width of one sheet of paper and not a centimetre more. The train left at nine minutes past ten, four minutes late, and we are still crawling out through the back of the city at a speed I could match on a bicycle. I promised you a letter from every night train, so here is the first one, and you will have to forgive the handwriting, because the table moves whenever we cross a set of points.

The compartment is smaller than I remembered and nicer than I expected. There is a narrow bed already made up with a white sheet and a grey blanket, a tiny basin that folds out of the wall, a mirror, a reading lamp, and a window with a blind that pulls down from the top. The conductor, a tall woman with a silver badge and a voice like a radio announcer, brought me a bottle of water and a paper bag with tomorrow’s breakfast in it, and asked whether I wanted to be woken at the border. I said yes. I would hate to sleep through it.

Out of the window the city is going by backwards, the way you only ever see it from a train. Not the fronts of things but the backs: yards full of bicycles, kitchen windows with the lights on, a man on a balcony in a vest smoking and watching us pass, a wall covered in names. A tram stopped at a crossing with three people inside, all looking at their phones. A girl walking a dog that refused to be walked. For a few minutes we ran alongside the river, and the lights of the bridges were stretched out on the water like long yellow stitches.

I think this is my favourite part of any journey. Not the arriving, and not the place itself, but the hour when you have left and nothing is expected of you. Nobody can call me. Nobody needs me to decide anything. The train will go where it goes and I will be on it, and for ten hours my only job is to notice things.

You asked me once why I prefer trains to planes when planes are so much quicker. I gave you some sensible answer about the airport, and the queues, and the luggage, and all of that is true. But the real answer is this hour. On a plane you leave one place and arrive in another, and in between there is nothing, just a tube of air at thirty thousand feet. On a train the places in between are real. You see where one city stops and the fields start. You feel the land change under you.

We have stopped now, at a small station I did not catch the name of. There is one lamp on the platform and a bench under it and nobody waiting. A man in a high-visibility jacket is walking along the train with a torch, shining it at the wheels, listening to something only he can hear. Every so often he knocks on a wheel with a hammer, a clear high note like a bell, and moves on.

My neighbour in the next compartment is an old man travelling with a violin case. I met him in the corridor when we were both looking for the conductor. He told me he is going to play at his granddaughter’s wedding, the day after tomorrow, and that he has been practising the same piece for three months, and that he is still not sure he can get through it without crying. I told him nobody at a wedding minds a crying violinist. He laughed and said I clearly had not met his daughter.

The train is moving again. The lamp and the bench slide away, and then there is nothing outside but dark, and every so often a single lit window in a farmhouse a long way off, like a ship at sea. I have turned off the big light and left the reading lamp on, so that I can see out.

I will write again in the morning, from the border, if the table and the train allow it. Look after the plants, and do not let the cat convince you that she has not been fed.

With love from somewhere in the middle,

Ada
